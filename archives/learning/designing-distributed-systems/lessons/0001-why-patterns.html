Lesson 01 · Part I, Chapter 1

Why Patterns Win Interviews

The whole reason this book exists — and the single mental shift that makes you sound senior in a design round.

Your mission: reason fluently in system design interviews. This lesson gives you the frame the rest of the book hangs on. Every later pattern is a named tool you'll pull off this shelf.

The one idea

Burns' argument in Chapter 1 is simple: we already did this once. Single-machine programming got dramatically easier when we stopped reinventing everything and started sharing three things — patterns (named solutions), practices (repeatable ways of working), and components (reusable building blocks). Sorting, hashing, linked lists — nobody rewrites those. You name them and move on.

Distributed systems are roughly where single-machine code was decades ago: everyone hand-rolls the same load balancers, queues, and retry loops from scratch. The book's whole project is to give distributed systems the same shared vocabulary. (Ch. 1, O'Reilly)

Why this is an interview superpower An interview is a language test as much as a design test. A candidate who says "I'd put a sidecar next to it for TLS termination" lands harder than one who describes the same thing in five vague sentences. Naming the pattern signals you've seen the problem before. That's exactly the shared language Chapter 1 is selling.
"Rather than build this from scratch, I'd reach for a well-known pattern here — it's a solved shape, so let me name it and spend our time on the tradeoffs."

The map you're about to fill in

The book groups its patterns into three families. Hold this map — every future lesson slots into one box.

Single-node
Multiple containers cooperating on one machine: sidecar, ambassador, adapter. Small, cheap, high-leverage.
Serving
Handling live user traffic at scale: replicated/load-balanced, sharded, scatter/gather, functions, ownership election.
Batch
Chewing through bulk work, not live requests: work queues, event-driven batch, coordinated batch.

(The 2nd edition adds universal concerns on top — observability, AI inference, and failure patterns. We'll get there.)
